Large UAE organisations — banks, government-related entities, airlines, energy and real estate groups — increasingly want their own security operations centre. Some are driven by regulation, some by scale, some by a desire to control their own data. Building a SOC that works, though, is a multi-year programme, not a procurement exercise.

## The three things a SOC is made of

### People

A 24/7 SOC needs enough staff to cover every hour of the year. Once leave, training and attrition are accounted for, one seat around the clock takes roughly five people. A modest SOC with two analysts on shift at all times, plus team leads, engineers and a manager, quickly reaches double-digit headcount — in one of the most competitive talent markets in the region.

### Process

The difference between a functioning SOC and a room full of screens is process: triage standards, escalation paths, playbooks for common incident types, shift handovers, case management and metrics. These need writing, testing and continual updating.

### Technology

A SIEM, EDR, a case management or SOAR platform, threat intelligence and secure analyst workstations — integrated so analysts are not switching between ten consoles. Tooling is the easiest part to buy and the easiest to over-buy.

## Measure maturity before and after

We use **SOC-CMM**, a widely used SOC maturity model, to score a SOC across business, people, process, technology and services domains. An honest baseline shows where investment will make the biggest difference, and a repeat assessment proves progress to leadership.

## Use cases first, tools second

The best SOCs start from the threats that matter — identity compromise, ransomware, data theft, attacks on edge devices — and build detection use cases and response playbooks for each, mapped to MITRE ATT&CK. Tools are then chosen to support those use cases.

## The hybrid model

Many organisations in Dubai choose a co-managed SOC: an internal team during the working week, and a partner covering nights, weekends, public holidays and overflow on the same platform. It gives control and local knowledge without needing to staff every hour internally from day one — and it is a sensible stepping stone toward a fully in-house operation.

## Typical timeline

- **Months 1–2:** maturity assessment, target operating model and design.
- **Months 2–4:** platform deployment, use cases and playbooks.
- **Months 4–6:** staffing, training, tabletop exercises and transition.
- **Ongoing:** quarterly maturity reviews and continuous improvement.
